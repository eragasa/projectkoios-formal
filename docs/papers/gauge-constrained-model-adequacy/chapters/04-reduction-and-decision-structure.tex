\chapter{Admissible-Set Compatibility, Certificates, and Model Selection}
\label{ch:reduction-decision}

\section{Returning to the full reduction problem}

The preceding chapters developed the operator branch of the adequacy problem: retained
spaces, coordinate alignment, aligned differences, Fourier representation, and
truncation.  We now restore the spectral criterion and the candidate-model quantifier.
For each declared model class $\mathfrak M_j$, the question is whether the spectral and
operator criteria accept one common Hamiltonian.

Let $\Theta_j$ be the parameter domain and let
\[
  \Phi_j:\Theta_j\longrightarrow\mathfrak M_j,
  \qquad \theta\longmapsto H_j(\theta)
\]
be the candidate map.  The parameterization need not be injective, but the losses and
model metric must be explicit about whether they act on parameters or Hamiltonians.  A
parameter-space distance is not automatically a Hamiltonian-space distance, and two
parameter vectors representing the same Hamiltonian must not be treated as physically
distinct unless that distinction is intended.

Given a spectral loss $\mathcal L_{E,j}$, an aligned operator loss
$\mathcal L_{H,j}$, an alignment family $\mathcal U_j$, and tolerances $\tau_E$ and
$\tau_H$, define
\[
  \mathfrak A_{E,j}
  =\{H_j(\theta):\mathcal L_{E,j}(\theta)\leq\tau_E\}
\]
and
\[
  \mathfrak A_{H,j}
  =\left\{H_j(\theta):
      \inf_{C\in\mathcal U_j}\mathcal L_{H,j}(C,\theta)
      \leq\tau_H
    \right\}.
\]
The exact model-adequacy proposition is
\[
  \operatorname{Compatible}(j)
  \;:\!\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \mathfrak A_{E,j}\cap\mathfrak A_{H,j}\neq\varnothing.
\]
When the alignment infimum is attained, unfolding the definitions gives the coupled
witness structure
\[
  \exists\theta\in\Theta_j,\ \exists C\in\mathcal U_j,
  \quad
  \mathcal L_{E,j}(\theta)\leq\tau_E
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \mathcal L_{H,j}(C,\theta)\leq\tau_H.
\]
Without attainment, membership defined by an infimum at the threshold boundary need not
supply an alignment realizing that bound; an attainment theorem or an explicitly
relaxed approximate-witness statement is then required.  This distinction is essential
in an exact treatment.

The checked finite compatibility schema now packages this quantifier structure at a
generic level.  A value of
\decl{FiniteCompatibilitySchema} supplies a finite model inventory, a finite candidate
inventory for each model, spectral and aligned-operator losses with model-relative
budgets, a candidate-relative alignment family, and a finite inventory of mandatory
operator-channel predicates.  Losses take values in the extended nonnegative reals so
that the alignment infimum retains the empty-family boundary.  Central operator
acceptance additionally requires the declared alignment family to be nonempty.

The declaration \decl{Compatible} uses the alignment-infimum criterion and literal
intersection of spectral and operator admissible sets.  The stronger declaration
\decl{WitnessCompatible} requires one common candidate and one alignment that lies in
the declared family, passes the operator budget, and satisfies every mandatory channel.
Thus the formal API does not infer a minimizing alignment from threshold-boundary
membership.  Theorems \decl{BG-DECISION-THM-017--023} separate infimum bounds,
attainment, and witness promotion; \decl{BG-DECISION-THM-024--026} identify central and
witness compatibility and prove only the justified implication from the stronger notion
to the central one.

This generic assembly does not instantiate $\Theta_j$, $\Phi_j$, either scientific loss,
the budgets, supports, or channel contracts for silicon or any KSD calculation.  It is
therefore an exact schema for the end-to-end finite logic, not an end-to-end scientific
adequacy theorem or runtime certificate.

\section{Spectral and operator criteria are different predicates}

The spectral criterion acts on selected spectral data.  Depending on the application,
it may compare ordered eigenvalues, gaps, valley locations, effective masses, or other
derived quantities.  The finite formal basis includes exact rank-two eigenvalue
formulas, ordered-band perturbation bounds, and spectral-residual expansions under
explicit hypotheses.  These results justify particular finite spectral inequalities;
they do not validate a numerical eigensolver or establish that a selected observable is
the scientifically appropriate one.

The operator criterion acts on matrix information after alignment.  A representative
real-space loss has the form
\[
  \mathcal L_{H,j}(C,\theta)
  =\sum_R\omega_R
    \left\|
      H_{\mathrm{ref}}(R)
      -C\,H_j(R;\theta)\,C^\dagger
    \right\|_{\mathrm F}^2,
\]
with the direction of $C$ adjusted consistently with the adopted pullback convention.
More structured versions may use momentum-dependent alignments, shell decompositions,
or additional symmetry constraints.  The weights, retained translations, norm, and
alignment family are all part of the predicate.

Spectral equality does not imply operator equality.  Unitary conjugation preserves a
matrix spectrum while changing its entries, and different real-space Hamiltonians may
agree on a selected finite spectral inventory.  Conversely, a small weighted operator
residual does not automatically bound every derived spectral quantity unless a theorem
connects the chosen operator norm and observable.  The two admissible sets must
therefore remain separate until their intersection is tested.

\section{Common-witness semantics}

The intersection formulation prevents three invalid substitutions.

First, independently fitted minimizers do not supply a common endpoint.  If
$H_E\in\mathfrak A_{E,j}$ and $H_H\in\mathfrak A_{H,j}$ but $H_E\neq H_H$, then the two
successes show only that each set is nonempty.

Second, separately chosen alignments cannot be combined unless the proposition permits
them.  A joint operator criterion requiring one common $C$ must be witnessed by that
same $C$ for every operator objective it contains.

Third, a favorable support or cutoff selected for one residual channel cannot be paired
with a different support selected for another channel when the scientific claim asks
for one reduced Hamiltonian.  Candidate, alignment, support, and threshold witnesses
must be shared exactly as specified by the predicate.

These are logical restrictions rather than optimization preferences.  No amount of
post-processing can turn incompatible witnesses into the existential witness required
by the intersection.

\section{Channelwise operator admissibility}
\label{sec:channelwise-operator-admissibility}

The three-channel calculus of
Section~\ref{sec:three-channel-residual-calculus} refines the operator predicate without
changing the outer common-Hamiltonian question.  For a candidate parameter $\theta$, an
alignment $C$, and a support choice $A$, suppose the contract constructs one aligned
interpolant $H_{\mathrm{int}}(C,\theta)$ and one truncation of that same field
$H_{\mathrm{tr}}(C,\theta,A)$.  The associated channel losses may be written
\[
  e_{\mathrm{int}}(C,\theta),\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{trunc}}(C,\theta,A),\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{total}}(C,\theta,A),
\]
using one declared pointwise, maximum, or root-mean-square norm.  The exact calculus
then supplies
\[
  e_{\mathrm{total}}(C,\theta,A)
  \leq
  e_{\mathrm{int}}(C,\theta)
  +e_{\mathrm{trunc}}(C,\theta,A).
\]
It does not make the three reported scalars additive.

An application may require separate budgets
\[
  e_{\mathrm{int}}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{int}},\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{trunc}}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{trunc}},\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{total}}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{total}}.
\]
All mandatory inequalities must then hold for the same $\theta$, $C$, $A$, intermediate
field, and evaluation inventory.  Separate minimizers of the three losses establish
only separate existential statements.  In particular, the minimum interpolation error
plus the minimum truncation error need not bound the total error of any one candidate.

The triangle theorem can propagate component budgets to a total budget when
$\tau_{\mathrm{int}}+\tau_{\mathrm{trunc}}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{total}}$ and the witnesses
are conformant.  The converse does not hold: a small total error can coexist with large
component errors because of cancellation.  A decision contract may therefore retain
all three channels to distinguish numerical interpolation quality from deliberate
model reduction even when only the total field enters the outer operator admissible
set.

The exact Fourier-tail results specialize $e_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ only.  Conversion,
projection, disentanglement, localization, alignment construction, finite-size, and
parent-model errors require additional contracts and cannot be charged to that Fourier
tail by relabeling.

\section{Set distance and exact incompatibility}

Let $X_j$ be a declared metric space containing both admissible sets, with metric
$d_j$.  Their separation is
\[
  \delta_j^*
  =\inf_{\substack{H_E\in\mathfrak A_{E,j}\\
                    H_H\in\mathfrak A_{H,j}}}
     d_j(H_E,H_H).
\]
When either admissible set is empty, the application must declare how separation is
represented; ordinary infimum notation over an empty product does not by itself provide
the intended diagnostic.  Nonemptiness is therefore a substantive premise in the exact
set-distance theorems used here.

If the sets intersect, their distance is zero.  A certified positive lower bound proves
disjointness.  The implication from zero distance back to intersection is not valid for
arbitrary sets.  It becomes valid in the checked finite geometries through explicit
attainment, compactness, closedness, or direct algebraic characterization.

This distinction resolves an ambiguity that can otherwise enter informal model-
adequacy language.  There are at least three different conclusions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item literal compatibility: $\mathfrak A_{E,j}\cap\mathfrak A_{H,j}\neq\varnothing$;
  \item exact zero separation under hypotheses that make zero distance decisive; and
  \item tolerance-level map compatibility:
        $\delta_j^*\leq\tau_{\mathrm{map}}$.
\end{enumerate}

The third is an application-defined relaxation of the first.  It is not a theorem that
a nonzero map tolerance produces one Hamiltonian satisfying both original losses.

The finite compatibility schema now closes the zero-distance converse under its exact
inventory hypotheses.  Both central admissible sets are subsets of the declared finite
candidate inventory, hence are finite and compact in any metric candidate carrier.  If
both sets are additionally nonempty, their extended distance is attained at one
spectral-admissible candidate and one operator-admissible candidate, and
\[
  \delta_j^*=0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \operatorname{Compatible}(j),
  \qquad
  0<\delta_j^*
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \neg\operatorname{Compatible}(j).
\]
The nonemptiness premises are substantive: if either central admissible set is empty,
the extended infimum retains the value $\top$ rather than reporting a finite attained
separation.  These finite-inventory equivalences do not apply to an undeclared continuum
of candidates and do not turn a positive map tolerance into literal intersection.

\section{Exact finite loss geometry}

Several checked geometries instantiate this general picture.  In a Hilbert space,
closed balls with centers $c_1,c_2$ and radii $r_1,r_2$ intersect exactly when
\[
  \lVert c_1-c_2\rVert\leq r_1+r_2.
\]
When the balls are disjoint, their set distance is
\[
  \lVert c_1-c_2\rVert-r_1-r_2.
\]
This supplies a representation-independent compatibility theorem once the two losses
have been proved equivalent to distances from the relevant centers.

The weighted Frobenius layer establishes nonnegativity, Cauchy--Schwarz, polarization,
invariance under common unitary conjugation, and expansions of spectral and operator
residuals.  Under centering and common-power hypotheses, matrix-valued comparisons
reduce to scalar geometric data.  Those hypotheses are not automatic consequences of
using a Frobenius norm.

For canonical Hermitian $2\times2$ families, admissible regions become explicit ellipses
or rectangles in coefficient space.  Their intersections and exact set distances can
be evaluated symbolically.  The same representation gives an exact bound from
unnormalized entrywise Frobenius matrix error to ordered-band error.

These results demonstrate how an instance of the spectral--operator compatibility
problem can become exactly decidable.  They are bounded specializations, not a proof
that every model class has elliptical loss geometry or that every runtime admissible
set is compact and exhaustively represented.

\section{Exact certificates}

A certificate is finite data together with a checker and a theorem connecting checker
acceptance to a mathematical proposition.  In the rank-two geometry, the certificate
families verify exact witnesses, spectral enclosures, containment in admissible regions,
and strict separation.  Box certificates replace a single approximate point by a
product of exact endpoint intervals and prove uniform conclusions for every point in
the box under the encoded endpoint hypotheses.

For the central adequacy problem, a positive certificate may establish one common
candidate in both admissible sets.  A negative certificate must establish separation of
the full declared sets, not merely failure of two optimizer outputs to coincide.  The
checker proposition must identify the model class, losses, tolerances, metric,
alignment family, and candidate-domain coverage to which its conclusion applies.

A separate finite Pareto layer checks dominance, threshold feasibility, and membership
in an efficient mask for one supplied finite table of objective values.  Its soundness
concerns that table.  It does not establish that the table contains every scientifically
relevant candidate, alignment, support, or model class.

The logical direction of a certificate theorem is crucial.  Checker acceptance may
imply a precise proposition because the checker consumes data satisfying an exact
contract.  The theorem does not show that arbitrary runtime output satisfies that
contract.  A runtime bridge must additionally freeze the schema, reject malformed or
unknown data, justify exact or outward-rounded inputs, retain provenance, and
independently verify the producer.  Serialization integrity authenticates bytes; it
does not convert floating-point values into exact enclosures.

\section{Three-way interval decisions}

Interval analysis supplies the broader enclosure framework for certified statements
about quantities known only through bounds~\cite{MooreKearfottCloud2009}. The decision
semantics below are the exact, application-facing specialization checked by the Formal
declarations.

Suppose an exact nonnegative quantity $q$ is enclosed by $[\ell,u]$ and the acceptance
budget is $\tau$.  A sound upper-bound decision has three substantive cases:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{accepted} when the interval is valid and $u\leq\tau$;
  \item \emph{rejected} when the interval is valid and $\tau<\ell$; and
  \item \emph{unresolved} when the valid interval overlaps the threshold.
\end{enumerate}

The unresolved case records that the supplied information does not decide the exact
comparison.  Replacing it by acceptance or rejection would add information absent from
the enclosure.

Malformed endpoint order is handled fail-closed: the checker classifies it as rejected.
That classification is structural rather than substantive.  A malformed interval
cannot contain an exact value, so no conclusion that the underlying quantity exceeds
the budget follows without a separate containment premise.

For the spectral--operator problem, joint acceptance requires both criteria to accept
for the same candidate and all required operator channels to accept for the same
alignment and support.  One substantively rejected objective is sufficient for joint
rejection of that candidate.  Otherwise, any unresolved mandatory objective leaves the
joint result unresolved.  Candidate-level rejection is not model-class infeasibility
unless the candidate inventory is complete or a global separation theorem covers the
class.

\section{Selection over a finite model hierarchy}

Let $J$ be the finite ordered inventory of tested model classes.  A class $j\in J$ is
the first compatible class when
\[
  \operatorname{Compatible}(j)
\]
and every earlier declared class is incompatible.  The definition inspects the finite
order directly and therefore requires no monotonicity assumption.

This finite first-passage construction matches the model-selection question in the
extended abstract: identify the smallest tested class supporting a common spectral and
operator endpoint.  The word ``tested'' is essential.  The result is relative to the
inventory $J$, its ordering, and the exact compatibility predicate.  It does not select
the smallest member of every possible Hamiltonian family.

If class inclusions and criterion conformity have been proved, feasibility may transfer
forward through the hierarchy.  The formal
\decl{ConformantModelEmbedding} does not encode nesting by name alone: it requires an
injective candidate map and explicit preservation of candidate membership, spectral
passage, alignment-family nonemptiness, alignment-infimum passage, and full operator
witnesses.  Theorems \decl{BG-DECISION-THM-029--030} then transfer central and stronger
witness compatibility separately.  Without such data, the finite decision must check
each class independently.

The declarations \decl{BG-DECISION-THM-031--033} instantiate the existing first-passing
logic with central compatibility.  They prove existence when at least one declared class
passes, uniqueness, and the expanded condition that every earlier inventory member
fails.  Likewise, the maximum of separately selected first-passing spectral and
operator classes need not be a joint first-passing class unless a theorem shows that
their witnesses can be made common.

\section{Additional exact finite reductions}

Two additional theorem families illustrate the same discipline.  For a rank-one
perturbation of an invertible finite operator, determinant and Sherman--Morrison
identities reduce the perturbed problem to a scalar secular factor.  A supplied exact
zero gives a kernel vector under explicit invertibility and denominator hypotheses.
The algebra does not prove existence or uniqueness of a root or convergence to an
infinite-volume defect state.

For finite gauge loops, an ordered product of link matrices transforms by conjugation at
the base point.  Determinants are invariant under the supplied closed-loop gauge action,
and rank-one projector products admit an exact Bargmann-product interpretation.  These
identities do not establish continuum topology, global phase unwrapping, Chern
integrality, or a Wannier obstruction.

The governing principle is the same as for model adequacy: an exact finite result is
indexed by its domain, hypotheses, comparison maps, and decision contract.  It gains no
additional scientific scope merely by being machine checked.
